Abstract
This paper investigates the motion of a projectile during flight from an aerody-
namic perspective, considering the effects of air resistance, gravity, and wind speed.
The dynamic equations for the projectile were established and solved numerically.
By analyzing the initial state of the artillery shell, all feasible solutions were deter-
mined for stationary targets. Subsequent discussions focused on estimating wind
speeds for non-stationary targets, establishing empirical formulas that enable ar-
tillery units to achieve precise and rapid strikes without relying on computers or
calculators.
When the target is fixed, using the scenario set in the first question with a
horizontal range difference of 1200 meters between the target and artillery at the
same elevation, the feasible solution ranges for θ and v are (3, 77) degrees and (147,
450) m/s, respectively. The minimum time required is 3.7 seconds, corresponding to
v = 450 m/s and θ = 3°.Then incorporate the effect of wind speed on air resistance.
Investigate how the selection of initial angle is influenced by varying wind speeds at
different heights and locations, while keeping the initial velocity fixed at 250 m/s.
An empirical formula for estimation was fitted using the least squares method. The
R-squared values are 0.99, 0.99, and 0.687, respectively, indicating excellent model
fit.
After testing the empirical formula using randomly generated targets and wind
speeds, the initial angle prediction deviated by approximately 0.05 degrees from
the actual value under windless conditions. Under windy conditions, the initial
angle prediction deviated by approximately 4 degrees from the actual value.
Key Words: Ballistics; Artillery; Launch Angle Estimation; Air Resistance;
Wind Correction; Linear Fitting

Introduction
1.1
Background
In early 19th-century warfare, artillery served as a pivotal force on the battlefield.
Based on black powder propulsion, these cannons fired spherical iron projectiles typically
weighing 5 kilograms with a diameter of 11 centimeters, achieving muzzle velocities up to
450 meters per second. Widely deployed 150 to 200 years ago, artillery oﬀicers’ primary
duty was rapidly determining firing parameters—namely muzzle velocity and elevation
angle—based on target location and environmental factors like elevation and wind speed
to ensure accurate projectile impact.
Ballistics is the science of projectile motion.
In idealized models, the trajectory
follows a standard parabola, with range primarily determined by the projectile’s initial
velocity and launch elevation angle. However, in real-world shooting scenarios, air resis-
tance significantly impacts the motion of spherical projectiles. It not only causes velocity
decay but also directly reduces range and lowers the apogee height. The magnitude of
air resistance is primarily related to the projectile’s flight speed, external shape, and
atmospheric density.
This study aims to develop a practical method enabling artillery units to rapidly
estimate the initial velocity and velocity angle of fired projectiles using empirical formu-
las. This approach avoids solving complex differential equations in dynamics, thereby
significantly enhancing both the accuracy and speed of target engagement.

1.2
Problem Restatement
Consider a cannon from 150 or 200 years ago that fires spherical projectiles with a
mass of 5 kg and a diameter of 11 cm, achieving an initial velocity of up to 450 m/s. If the
target is 1,200 meters away horizontally, and both the cannon and the target are located
500 meters above sea level, determine the initial velocity and elevation angle (relative to
the horizontal plane) required to hit the target.
1.3
Problem Analyze
Regarding the first question, when the firing point and target are at the same ele-
vation, the distance between the projectile and the target point is calculated using the
Euclidean distance. If the distance between the two is less than 10 meters, the target is
deemed to have been hit.
Regarding the second question, to enable artillery oﬀicers to rapidly estimate the
muzzle velocity and elevation angle required to hit targets, we developed a model based
on ballistic principles and linear approximation methods. Considering historical context,
artillery muzzle velocity was typically controlled by charge quantity, allowing for two
modes: half-charge (velocity approx. 250 m/s) and full-charge (velocity approx. 450
m/s).
Since a velocity of 450 m/s requires longer time to strike targets at different
horizontal elevations, the half-charge mode at 250 m/s is employed. Elevation angles
are mechanically adjusted via the gun mount within a range of 0° to 45°. While precise
calculation of ballistic trajectories affected by air resistance is complex, we observed an
approximate linear relationship between elevation angle and either range or elevation
difference within a certain range. Therefore, linear fitting is used for estimation.

2.1
Assumptions
• Assume the projectile is solid with uniform density distribution.
• Assume the projectile is perfectly smooth.
• Assume the air is uniform and remains at standard temperature and pressure during
flight, as air density varies with temperature and pressure.
• Neglect the effect of supersonic speeds on the drag coeﬀicient.
• Neglect the projectile’s spin phenomenon.

velocity is controlled by the charge quantity) and the need for model simplification, only
two common modes of initial velocity are considered: half charge (approximately 250

of 450 m/s cannot cover the 1000–1500 m target range, and the time taken for the pro-

3.2.2
Wind Speed Correction
Wind conditions can be either headwind or tailwind.
These two scenarios exert
opposite effects on the distance to the shell’s impact point. Headwind, where the wind
direction opposes the projectile’s trajectory, continuously impedes its forward motion,
accelerating energy loss.
This ultimately results in the projectile landing closer than
it would have in calm conditions. Tailwind, where the wind direction aligns with the
projectile’s flight path, reduces energy dissipation, enabling the projectile to travel farther
than it would under windless conditions.

Strengths and Weaknesses
4.1
Strengths
1 We systematically addressed the issue. In developing the estimation method, we
comprehensively considered multiple critical factors such as the target’s horizontal
position, elevation angle, and initial velocity selection, designing solutions tailored
to the practical operational scenarios of artillery units.
2 Our model is highly practical. By establishing a linear empirical model linking ele-
vation angle to horizontal range/altitude, artillery units can rapidly and effectively
estimate firing parameters for targets within a horizontal range of 1000 to 1500
meters and relative to the artillery position at altitudes ranging from -178 to 778
meters—all without complex calculations on the battlefield.
3 Our solution demonstrates high feasibility. Using the method of controlled variables
and least-squares fitting, we derived a universal relationship based on a typical
scenario with a horizontal position of 1200 meters and a relative height of zero.
Its applicability within adjacent ranges was validated through linear interpolation,
confirming the robustness of the method.
4 Our assumptions and parameters align closely with historical context. The model
is constructed based on given projectile parameters (mass 5kg, diameter 11cm) and
the practicality of loading gunpowder into the cannon, specifying a typical initial
velocity (250m/s). All assumptions conform to the technical conditions of the late
18th and early 19th centuries.
4.2
Weaknesses
1 The model’s accuracy has inherent limitations. Despite our efforts to approximate
the true ballistic trajectory through linear fitting, the non-linear relationship be-
tween wind speed and elevation angle, coupled with the interaction between wind
speed and target position, results in certain model errors. The first shot may require
a minor adjustment based on observation.
2 Certain environmental factors have been omitted. To ensure the model remains
suﬀiciently simplified for rapid battlefield deployment, we have disregarded fac-
tors such as air turbulence and projectile spin. In practical applications, this may
necessitate additional compensation by oﬀicers based on their experience.
3 The data sources are somewhat idealized. The model construction heavily relies on
the specified projectile parameters and initial velocity. If batch variations exist in

Team 155
Page 16 of 20
the actual propellant charge, weight, or shape of the ammunition used, this may
affect the accuracy of the model estimates.
5
Conclusion
After analyzing the first question, we identified all feasible solutions capable of hitting
a stationary target: θ ranges from 3 to 77 degrees, and v ranges from 143 to 450 m/s.
We then determined the trajectory that precisely hits the target among these feasible
solutions. The solution with the shortest duration is selected as the optimal one: t = 3.7
s, v = 450 m/s, θ= 3°.
Building upon the first question, we designed a rapid aiming method for artillery
based on a linear empirical model.
The problem was first divided into windless and
windy conditions. Using the control variable method, we obtained extensive data for the
reference point (1200, 0). We then employed least-squares fitting to derive an empirical
formula. After observing the target’s relative position (x, y) relative to the reference point
(1200, 0) and the approximate wind speed at that moment, artillery crews can substitute
these values into the fitted empirical formula to calculate the required elevation angle for
the gun.
In short, if the artillery adopts our strategy, it will be highly likely to strike the
target quickly and accurately.
